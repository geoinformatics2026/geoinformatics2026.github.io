# Do better street configurations mean higher land prices?  Nonlinear and spatially heterogeneous effects revealed by GeoShapley

**Author Information**  
Quang Cuong Doan<sup>1</sup>, Thi Cam Ngoc Bui<sup>2</sup>


<sup>1</sup> The University of Hong Kong  

<sup>2</sup> Vietnam National University  



## Abstract

Street configuration is a fundamental aspect of urban transport systems that influences accessibility, movement patterns, and spatial interaction. Previous studies have examined the link between spatial accessibility and house prices under linear assumptions, but most rely on linear assumptions and overlook the combined roles of nonlinearity and spatial heterogeneity in transport networks. To address this gap, the present study investigates how street configuration influences land prices through nonlinear and spatially varying mechanisms, using Hanoi as a case study. We integrate machine learning with GeoShapley to disentangle the marginal and location-specific effects of multiple street configuration measures. The findings indicate that street configuration contributes substantially to land-price prediction, with notable spatial heterogeneity. We observe that improved accessibility does not always translate into higher land prices; instead, land-price premiums peak within specific connectivity thresholds and may decline beyond them. Among configuration measures, local connectivity has the strongest effect, followed by betweenness and degree centrality, highlighting the importance of fine-grained street networks for transport accessibility. GeoShapley analysis reveals that geographic context alone explains 14.52% of land-price variation after accounting for all covariates, emphasising the limitations of linear and globally averaged models. The marginal effects of features on land prices vary across the study area, underlining the interaction between geographic context and land price determinants. These insights provide valuable guidance for transport-oriented land-use planning, especially in designing street hierarchies and accessibility-driven development strategies in rapidly urbanising cities.
