# Spatiotemporal Patterns and Driving Factors of Compound Heatwave and Air Pollution Hazards in the US

**Author Information**  
Wenrui Xu<sup>1</sup>, Lu Liang<sup>1</sup>


<sup>1</sup> University of California, Berkeley  



## Abstract

Two global crises, climate change and air pollution, are increasingly intertwined through more frequent and prolonged compound heatwave and air pollution events. Extreme temperature and poor air quality often overlap spatially and temporally, occurring either simultaneously or with short time lags, which amplify public health risks beyond what either hazard would cause alone. However, most existing research and environmental management practices continue to address heat and air pollution as separate issues. This research investigates the correlation between heat and haze, as well as the long-term cooccurrence trends in compound heatwave-pollution hazards, using hourly observations of temperature and six major air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, Ozone, NO2, SO2, and CO) from 2008 to 2025 across 3,437 EPA monitoring stations in the United States. The results indicate that the frequency of compound hazards has increased over the past decades, with high-risk areas concentrated in California’s Central Valley, around the Great Lakes, and in major metropolitan regions along the East Coast. The correlation between heat and haze exhibits marked spatial heterogeneity across climate regions and between urban and rural settings. Distinct pollutant-specific patterns are also observed, with NO2 showing predominantly negative correlations with temperature, while Ozone exhibits positive correlations. By moving beyond the traditional single-hazard paradigm, this study provides new insights into the dynamics and spatial patterns of multi-hazard interactions at a national scale over an extended temporal span, offering implications for future risk assessment and adaptive strategies.
