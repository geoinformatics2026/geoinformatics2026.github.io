# Citizen-Generated Geospatial Data in the Age of AI: Toward a New Research Agenda

**Author Information**  
Bin Li<sup>1</sup>


<sup>1</sup> Central Michigan University  



## Abstract

For more than two decades, volunteered geographic information, participatory mapping, and citizen sensing have expanded spatial knowledge production beyond state agencies and technical experts. The rapid integration of machine learning, computer vision, and generative AI into mapping platforms now fundamentally transforms this landscape which reshapes not only how citizen-generated data are analyzed, but how they are created, validated, governed, and reused. This paper explores the following question: what should a new research agenda for citizen-generated geospatial data look like in the age of AI? Drawing on a critical synthesis of scholarship in volunteered geographic information, GeoAI, citizen science, digital ethics, and platform governance, the paper argues that AI introduces a qualitative shift from citizen-generated data as a predominantly human-authored resource toward hybrid assemblages combining human input, AI assistance, and AI-generated spatial features. This shift raises urgent questions about provenance, data quality, uncertainty, bias, transparency, and accountability, while simultaneously opening new possibilities for participatory knowledge production, including AI-assisted mapping, real-time anomaly detection, and broader integration of local knowledge in environmental monitoring, disaster response, and urban governance. The paper proposes six priorities for a forward-looking research agenda: (1) provenance and labeling standards for AI-assisted geodata; (2) revised frameworks for assessing quality and uncertainty in hybrid data environments; (3) representational fairness and the mitigation of participation gaps; (4) human-AI collaboration in mapping workflows; (5) ethical governance, informed consent, and community data rights; and (6) interoperable infrastructures for sustainable data stewardship. Rather than positioning citizens merely as passive data providers for AI systems, the paper advances a human-centered approach in which communities actively shape the design, oversight, and public value of geospatial AI. By bridging debates that are often treated in isolation, this paper offers an interdisciplinary agenda for scholars and practitioners working at the intersection of mapping, participation, and artificial intelligence.
