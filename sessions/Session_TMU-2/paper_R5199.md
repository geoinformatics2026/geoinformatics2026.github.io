# Freight Network Embedding and Urban Transport CO₂ Exposure: Multi-stage Evidence from China

**Author Information**  
Runyu Miao<sup>1</sup>, Shifen Cheng<sup>1</sup>, Yibo Zhao<sup>1</sup>, Feng Lu<sup>1</sup>


<sup>1</sup> Institute of Geographic Sciences and Natural Resources Research, CAS  



## Abstract

Reducing transport-related carbon emissions has become a major challenge for achieving broader climate mitigation goals. However, it remains unclear whether high levels of urban transport CO₂ exposure mainly arise from local economic expansion or from the positions cities occupy within national freight networks. This study investigates this question using intercity road freight origin–destination flows in China, the MEIC transport-sector emission inventory, and socioeconomic data from the China City Statistical Yearbook. We develop a multi-stage analytical framework to examine the determinants of urban transport CO₂ exposure. The analysis combines correlation analysis, comparative tests, mechanism identification, and counterfactual network reconstruction. The results indicate that urban transport CO₂ exposure is not explained solely by local economic scale. Instead, it is shaped jointly by economic size and cities’ roles in freight networks. Population and economic activity remain the main basis for explaining differences in exposure across cities. At the same time, freight network strength, which measures the total freight connections of a city, provides a stable incremental explanation. This finding suggests that some cities experience additional exposure beyond what can be explained by economic scale alone. The analysis also shows that the influence of freight networks is conditional rather than universal. Network effects are most visible among medium- to large-scale cities and within the middle-to-upper range of the exposure distribution. Corridor analysis based on the physical road network further shows that transit freight flows contribute to high exposure in several hub cities. Counterfactual simulations demonstrate that reshaping intercity connections, while keeping local economic attributes unchanged, can significantly alter the configuration of highly exposed cities. Overall, the structure of intercity freight networks exerts an independent but uneven influence on urban transport CO₂ exposure. These findings suggest that mitigation policies should consider cross-regional freight interactions and corridor pressures rather than focusing only on city-level emission management.
