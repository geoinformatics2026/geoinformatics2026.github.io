# Evaluating Traditional and Novel Height-Diameter Models for Urban Tree DBH Estimation

**Author Information**  
Lan Qing Zhao<sup>1</sup>, Derek Robinson<sup>1</sup>


<sup>1</sup> University of Waterloo  



## Abstract

To quantify tree carbon stocks, researchers often rely on allometric equations, which relate biomass to structural attributes such as diameter at breast height (DBH) and tree height. Traditionally, accurately measuring tree height can be difficult compared to DBH; therefore, height-diameter (H-D) models were developed to estimate tree height from DBH and subsequently biomass. Remote sensing, particularly light detection and ranging (LiDAR), now provides an efficient and accurate means of measuring tree characteristics across large spatial extents. However, most H-D models are mostly derived from and for natural forests, resulting in a lack of equations tailored to urban environments. We present the results of local H-D models developed for urban trees in southern Ontario, Canada, using high-density LiDAR point clouds and in-situ measurements collected across seven study sites. Additionally, we explored the effects of incorporating canopy width into model development. Twenty frequently used equations from the literature were compiled and compared against our novel models to determine which models best estimated DBH and under what conditions. LiDAR data were collected using a Matrice 600 Pro and Harris Aerial H6 mounted with a Riegl miniVux1 and Ultra 120, acquired from Pheonix LiDAR Systems. Point clouds were acquired at different densities to evaluate the effect of point cloud density (300-42,000 pts/m2) on height and canopy width estimates. Point clouds were processed and manually segmented to extract height and canopy width metrics. Combined with in-situ DBH measurements, these data were used to develop novel local H-D models using supervised regression models. Model results were compared across three groups: literature-based H-D equations, novel H-D models, and novel models incorporating canopy width. Results show that novel models with canopy width achieved the highest DBH accuracy, followed by novel models using height alone, while literature-based equations have the lowest accuracy when applied to local urban trees.
