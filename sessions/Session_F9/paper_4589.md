# Opportunities and Biases of Digital Trace Data for Urban Development in the Global South

**Author Information**  
Wenlan Zhang<sup>1</sup>, Chen Zhong<sup>1</sup>


<sup>1</sup> University College London  



## Abstract

Digital trace data has emerged as a critical tool for urban planning. With the global trend of digitalisation, these emerging data sources and GeoAI techniques illuminate formal urban development in the Global South. This talk will showcase one of the applications we conducted in Nairobi, Kenya, where we mapped the spatial structure of visible waste accumulation as an indicator of sanitation inequality using street-level imagery. Applying a two-stage computer vision and vision–language pipeline to 306,420 Google Street View images, we identify 3,236 verified waste-pile locations with high detection precision (94.7%). Spatial analysis reveals pronounced and statistically significant clustering (Nearest Neighbour Ratio = 0.34; Z = −71.5), forming 32 persistent hotspots across the city. Waste exposure is strongly uneven: urban poor settlement areas occupy only 2.86% of Nairobi’s land area, yet contain 19.3% of all detections, with waste density more than 11 times higher than in other areas. Distance-decay modelling further shows that the probability of encountering visible waste declines sharply within 200 - 300 m of settlement boundaries, indicating concentrated environmental burden at the margins of urban poor communities. The result evidenced systematic disparities in waste management that are not captured by conventional administrative data miss. The approach offers a scalable framework for monitoring environmental exposure, identifying service gaps, and supporting more equitable urban infrastructure planning in data-scarce contexts. While data and digital services bring new potential to society, the collection and access to data from digitally invisible groups become critical issues that may intensify social-spatial inequalities. When directly applying research from the Global North to countries in the Global South, the methods used in these studies may create barriers that exclude digitally invisible groups from data-driven decision-making. Therefore, the talk will also address criticisms of bias in big data-based urban mappings.
