# Response and Risk Analysis of Shallow Groundwater and Surface Deformation to Extreme Rainfall in the Beijing Plain

**Author Information**  
Kan Wang<sup>1</sup>, Siyuan Cheng<sup>1</sup>, Xinyu Wan<sup>1</sup>, Lin Wang<sup>1</sup>, Mi Chen<sup>1</sup>, Xiaojuan Li<sup>1</sup>


<sup>1</sup> Capital Normal University  



## Abstract

Land subsidence in the Beijing Plain, driven by soil compression, poses significant threats to infrastructure. As global climate change intensifies extreme weather events, understanding the dynamic response mechanisms of shallow groundwater and surface deformation to extreme precipitation is critical. This study focuses on the Beijing Plain to explore the short-term response characteristics of shallow groundwater levels and surface deformation during extreme rainfall, providing scientific support for infrastructure risk assessment and resilient city construction. The research integrates daily precipitation records from over 120 stations, shallow groundwater depth monitoring data, and monthly time-series InSAR observation data from 2017 to 2025. The study first analyzes regional subsidence evolution and then specifically extracts data from the July 2023 catastrophic rainstorm and July 2024 heavy rainfall events to analyze the temporal correlation between groundwater levels and surface deformation driven by extreme precipitation. Findings indicate that subsidence rates in the Beijing Plain significantly slowed after 2022, with the average monthly deformation rate decreasing from -0.127 mm to -0.075 mm. During extreme rainfall, shallow groundwater levels exhibited significant short-term rises. In the July 2023 event (maximum daily rainfall of 181.4 mm), the average monthly rise was 1.72 m, with a peak rise of 10.17 m at Luozhuang Station. Consequently, the ground surface rebounded rapidly in the following month, reaching a local maximum rebound of 34.9 mm. A similar phenomenon occurred in 2024 with a local rebound of approximately 33.5 mm. Rapid groundwater fluctuations synchronize with surface deformation, creating large spatial deformation gradients that exacerbate differential subsidence and threaten sensitive engineering structures. While reduced groundwater extraction and recharging have mitigated subsidence, extreme precipitation triggers rapid surface rebounding. This coupling mechanism between hydrological changes and surface deformation requires further research to enhance geological disaster warnings and infrastructure resilience under climate change.
