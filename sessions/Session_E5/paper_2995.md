# Reassessing Urban Vibrancy: Discrepancies Between Nighttime Light– and Mobility-Based Estimates

**Author Information**  
Chun-Hsiang Chan<sup>1</sup>, Chia-Hsun Li<sup>1</sup>, Po-Yu Yeh<sup>1</sup>, Tzu-Cheng Chang<sup>2</sup>


<sup>1</sup> Department of Geography, National Taiwan Normal University  

<sup>2</sup> Department of Geography, National Taiwan University  



## Abstract

Urban vibrancy, the intensity and diversity of human activities, plays a central role in urban theory and spatial planning. Satellite-derived nighttime light (NTL) imagery has become a widely used proxy for vibrancy because of its global coverage and consistency. Yet artificial illumination may reflect infrastructural lighting rather than actual human presence, raising questions about the validity of light intensity as a behavioral indicator. Mobility data provide a more direct measure of human activity but remain spatially and institutionally constrained. Despite their widespread use, the measurement bias embedded in these two GIS-based proxies has not been systematically evaluated. This study comparatively assesses vibrancy estimates derived from mobility trajectories (private vehicles and taxis) and preprocessed NTL imagery with minimized streetlight effects. Local hot spot analysis identifies spatial clusters of overestimation and underestimation in NTL-based measures relative to mobility-based benchmarks. A generalized linear modeling framework further evaluates how land-use composition and point-of-interest distributions structure these discrepancies. Results show consistent overestimation of vibrancy in riverbank corridors, low-density residential zones, and science parks, where illumination intensity exceeds human mobility activity. Conversely, primary transportation corridors, especially in newly developed districts, are systematically underestimated despite substantial mobility flows. These patterns reveal structural measurement bias in widely adopted remote sensing proxies. This study contributes to GIScience and urban geography by advancing a spatially explicit diagnosis of proxy validity, demonstrating how data choice shapes urban inference, and promoting methodological accountability in GIS-based applications. By foregrounding measurement correctness and representational bias, the study encourages more critical and integrative uses of spatial big data. Future research should develop multi-source data fusion frameworks to improve the robustness and transferability of urban vibrancy indicators across heterogeneous urban contexts.
