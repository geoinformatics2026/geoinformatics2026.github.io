# Prenatal Exposure to PM₂.₅ and Ozone and Risk of Preterm Birth in Georgia (2015–2018): A Discrete-Time Survival Analysis Using FAQSD Data

**Author Information**  
Kabita Joshi<sup>1</sup>, Jun Tu<sup>2</sup>, Lili Yu<sup>1</sup>, Wei Yu<sup>1</sup>


<sup>1</sup> Georgia Southern Univesity  

<sup>2</sup> kennesaw.State University  



## Abstract

A large body of literature has reported associations between prenatal exposure to air pollution and adverse birth outcomes, including preterm birth. However, Epidemiologic evidence remains mixed, prior Georgia studies largely focused on Atlanta and earlier birth cohorts, and most relied on traditional regression approaches without considering time-varying exposure.  This study examines the association between ambient fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and ozone exposure, derived from Fused Air Quality Surface Using Downscaling (FAQSD) files, and the risk of preterm birth among singleton live births conceived in Georgia between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2018. FAQSD data are generated using a Bayesian space-time downscaler that fuses monitoring data with 12-km gridded CMAQ model output to produce daily predictions at 2010 U.S. Census tract centroids. We assess PM2.5 exposure across trimester 1, trimester 2, trimester 3, and total cumulative PM₂.₅ exposure. Ozone exposure is evaluated across corresponding gestational windows.  Time-varying exposures can arise either from short-term lagged variation due to seasonality in air pollution or from long-term cumulative exposure over pregnancy. Our model addresses this challenge by viewing gestational age as time-to-event data, where each pregnancy becomes at risk at a prespecified time (e.g., the 27th week). The pregnancy is then followed until either a birth occurs before the 37th week (preterm) or it reaches the 37th week (Full term). We define gestational weeks 28–36 as the at-risk period for preterm birth.  Discrete-time survival models adjust for maternal demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, tobacco use during pregnancy, marital status, birth order, infant sex, season of conception, and year of conception.  Results indicate that higher ambient PM2.5 and ozone exposure during pregnancy are associated with an increased risk of preterm birth, even in rural regions with relatively low air pollutant concentrations. These findings highlight the value of advanced spatiotemporal modeling in perinatal environmental health research.
