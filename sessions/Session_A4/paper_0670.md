# Geographical Differentiation of Land Use Evolution in Traditional Ethnic Villages from the Perspective of Traditional Ecological Views

**Author Information**  
Hongjiao Qu<sup>1</sup>, Jingbiao Yang<sup>1</sup>, Luo Guo<sup>1</sup>


<sup>1</sup> Minzu University of China  



## Abstract

Under global change, environmental changes driven by urbanization have profoundly reshaped human-environment interactions in rural areas. As micro-scale, coupled human-environment systems integrating culture, geography, and ecology, traditional ethnic villages show distinctive adaptive responses to external disturbances. Their differential response modes and internal mechanisms represent a key issue in human-environment system research. Existing studies focus mainly on macro-regional land use change, but lack systematic analysis of differentiated interaction patterns shaped by traditional ethnic ecological worldviews in village-surrounding geographic units. This study examines 4741 traditional villages in the Yangtze River Economic Belt, covering the Miao, Dong, Tujia, and Yi ethnic groups. Using 2 km and 4 km village buffers as analytical units, we integrate 1990–2020 remote sensing images, land use data, geographic data, and questionnaire surveys. GIS spatial analysis and landscape quantification are applied to explore dynamic responses and driving mechanisms. Results show that the macro-region experienced built-up land expansion, water area decline, and rising landscape fragmentation from 1990 to 2020. Within village buffers, however, these trends were strongly restructured by ethnic-specific interactions. Four response modes are identified: development-oriented (Dong), steady-state regulation (Miao and Tujia), and constrained adaptation (Yi). These differences stem from traditional ecological worldviews, resource ethics, and livelihood systems. Traditional spatial wisdom enhances landscape resilience and mitigates urbanization impacts. Based on 420 valid questionnaires, residents’ ecological perceptions and development demands are highly coupled with objective spatial changes and show significant ethnic differentiation. Respondents from all four groups express strong recognition of cultural protection and high willingness to participate in spatial planning. This study reveals the deep regulatory role of traditional ecological worldviews in micro-scale human-environment interactions under a changing environment, and provides a scientific geospatial basis for differentiated spatial governance of traditional villages in the Yangtze River Economic Belt.
