# A hybrid method for spatial prediction integrating the First and Third Laws of Geography

**Author Information**  
Guiming Zhang<sup>1</sup>


<sup>1</sup> Department of Geography & the Environment, University of Denver  



## Abstract

Spatial prediction involves estimating values of a target geographic variable at unsampled locations based on values of that variable observed at sampled locations. Spatial autocorrelation-based methods for spatial prediction (e.g., inverse distance weighting and ordinary kriging) rely on the First Law of Geography, which states that similar values of the target variable are expected at locations that are close to each other. More recently, environmental similarity-based approaches have emerged (e.g., individual predictive soil mapping; iPSM), drawing on the Third Law of Geography, which posits that locations with similar geographic configurations (e.g., environmental conditions as represented by a set of covariates) tend to exhibit similar values of the target variable. A key advantage of environmental similarity-based methods is their flexibility: they do not require specific numbers or spatial distributions of sample locations. However, these methods overlook geographic proximity, even though many geographic variables are shaped by both environmental similarity and spatial autocorrelation. This study introduces a hybrid spatial prediction method that integrates First and Third Laws of Geography, jointly leveraging environmental similarity and spatial autocorrelation principles within the ordinary kriging framework. In a case study predicting soil organic matter (SOM) concentration in Xuancheng, Anhui, China using 297 modeling soil samples and 61 validation soil samples on a 10 km x 10 km grid, the hybrid method outperformed approaches based solely on either environmental similarity or spatial autocorrelation as reflected in accuracy metrics including concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), root-mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE). This hybrid method offers a novel way of leveraging complementary geographical principles for spatial prediction.
