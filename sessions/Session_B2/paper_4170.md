# Neighbourhood Cycling Infrastructure, Commuting Behaviour, and Population Health in Australia: Evidence from the 2021 Census

**Author Information**

## Abstract

Active commuting is increasingly recognised as a key pathway linking urban infrastructure and population health. Yet, evidence on how neighbourhood cycling environments relate to commuting behaviour and long-term health outcomes in Australia remains limited. This study investigates whether neighbourhoods with more supportive cycling infrastructure are associated with higher cycling-to-work rates and differences in long-term health outcomes at the neighbourhood scale.  We integrate SA1-level data from the 2021 Census conducted by the Australian Bureau of Statistics, including Method of Travel to Work and self-reported long-term health conditions such as mental health conditions, arthritis, asthma, diabetes, heart disease, cancer, lung disease, stroke, kidney disease, and dementia. Neighbourhood cycling environments are quantified using multiple spatial indicators, including cycleway density, network connectivity, and accessibility to cycling routes, combined with socio-demographic and built-environment covariates to account for potential confounding.  To capture complex and potentially non-linear relationships between infrastructure, commuting behaviour, and health outcomes, we employ an explainable machine learning framework using Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Additionally, we compute spatially weighted cycling exposure using neighbouring SA1s and adjust health outcomes for the working-age population to partially mitigate ecological inference limitations.  This approach allows us to explore how neighbourhood infrastructure and commuting behaviour may be linked to patterns of long-term health conditions and to identify which infrastructure and contextual factors are most strongly associated with these patterns. The study highlights the potential for neighbourhood cycling-supportive environments to contribute to healthier population outcomes and provides a framework for integrating spatially detailed infrastructure and health data in urban planning and public health research.
