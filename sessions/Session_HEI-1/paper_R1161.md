# Geographic accessibility gains confer outsized cardiovascular benefits in underserved nations

**Author Information**  
Qiming Huang<sup>1</sup>, Bin Zhu<sup>1</sup>


<sup>1</sup> Southern University of Science and Technology  



## Abstract

Healthcare infrastructure is widely regarded as a key determinant of cardiovascular health, yet a systematic understanding of its global patterns of influence and underlying pathways remains limited. Integrating multi-source global data from 2014 to 2024, this study employs Bayesian mixed-effects models, piecewise structural equation modeling, and counterfactual scenario simulations to conduct a cross-national comparative analysis of the relationship between healthcare accessibility and cardiovascular disease mortality. Our findings reveal that the protective effect of healthcare accessibility on cardiovascular mortality exhibits significant diminishing marginal returns, with effect sizes heavily contingent on socioeconomic context: the strongest protective effect is observed in middle-income countries (β = -0.006, p < 0.001), whereas a "mechanism decoupling" phenomenon emerges in low-income countries (β = -0.003, p > 0.05). Disease-specific analyses indicate that stroke mortality is most sensitive to healthcare accessibility (β = -0.006, p < 0.001), while the association with hypertensive heart disease is comparatively weaker (β = 0.002, p > 0.05). Counterfactual simulations further suggest that increasing global healthcare accessibility to the 70th percentile could avert approximately 3.53 million cardiovascular deaths annually. Notably, strengthening primary healthcare systems yields greater health gains (3.72 million averted deaths) compared to merely expanding hospital infrastructure (2.27 million). This study provides a global-scale quantification of the structural contribution of healthcare infrastructure accessibility to disparities in cardiovascular mortality, advances the understanding of the causal chain linking social determinants, healthcare systems, and health outcomes, and offers an empirical foundation for reframing institutional explanations of global health inequalities.
