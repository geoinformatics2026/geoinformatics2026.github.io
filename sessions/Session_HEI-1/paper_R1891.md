# From Modeled to Realized Access: Comparing Travel Times to Mental Health Care Using Mobility Data in the United States

**Author Information**  
Wei Luo<sup>1</sup>, Yaxiong Shao<sup>1</sup>, M. Courtney Hughes<sup>1</sup>


<sup>1</sup> Northern Illinois University  



## Abstract

Access to mental health care remains a critical public health challenge in the United States, with geographic isolation and transportation barriers contributing to unmet needs. Traditional measures of spatial access—such as modeled travel time to the nearest provider—assume idealized travel conditions and may not reflect how people move through space in their daily lives. This study leverages mobile device-based mobility data from Advan to estimate realized travel times to mental health care facilities and compares them with conventional modeled travel times to better understand discrepancies in access and utilization. Using a national mental health facility database (2,888 matched in Advan), we calculate travel time from each Census block group to the nearest facility based on OpenStreetMap and standard road network assumptions. We then estimate realized travel times using aggregated and anonymized origin–destination visit data from the Advan Monthly Patterns dataset. By linking visitors’ home Census block groups to observed visits at identified facilities, we derive empirically observed travel times that reflect mobility behaviors. These realized times capture temporal, modal, and behavioral variability in travel. We evaluate differences between modeled and realized travel times across demographic, socioeconomic, and rural–urban gradients. We expect substantial discrepancies between realized travel times and modeled estimates, particularly in metropolitan areas with complex mobility patterns and in rural regions where provider scarcity and limited transportation options may increase travel burdens. We further examine how these discrepancies relate to mental health service utilization and potential indicators of unmet need. By introducing a data-driven approach to measuring travel burdens, this study highlights limitations of traditional spatial access metrics and underscores the importance of incorporating real-world mobility behavior into assessments of health care accessibility. The findings have implications for health services planning, transportation policy, and strategies aimed at reducing disparities in access to mental health care.
